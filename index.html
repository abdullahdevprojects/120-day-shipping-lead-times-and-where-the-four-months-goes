120 day shipping lead times and where the four months goes

Ocean transit is 25 to 40 days of it. Production, booking, customs and receiving take the rest, and Chinese New Year rearranges all of it once a year.

Updated September 2026  ·  Reading time about 4 minutes  ·  Topic: Logistics and import planning

Start date120-day windowDeadline

Counted from purchase order to warehouse shelf, not from the vessel departure date.

On this page

    Four months is the planning figure most importers use for a container of goods from Asia to Europe or North America, counted from purchase order to warehouse shelf. The ocean leg is the smallest part of it. Production, booking and customs take the rest.

    Where the 120 days actually goes

    Typical stages for a full container from a Chinese factory to a European warehouse
    StageTypical durationWho controls it
    Sampling and PO confirmation7 to 21 daysYou and the factory
    Production30 to 60 daysThe factory
    Inspection and booking7 to 14 daysYour QC firm and forwarder
    Ocean transit25 to 40 daysThe carrier
    Customs clearance1 to 10 daysYour broker and the authority
    Drayage and receiving3 to 10 daysTrucking and your warehouse

    Add it up and the midpoint sits close to 120 days. Most people planning a launch count only the transit time, quote themselves 30 days, and then discover that production has not started because the deposit cleared late.

    The variables that move the date most

    Chinese New Year

    The single largest scheduling factor in consumer goods. Factories close for one to three weeks, workers travel home, and output in the weeks either side drops sharply. Orders placed in December for February delivery routinely land in April. Plan around the date every year.

    Peak season and blank sailings

    August to October is peak for holiday inventory, and rates rise while space tightens. When demand falls, carriers cancel scheduled departures, which is called a blank sailing, and a cancelled sailing can add 7 to 14 days with no warning and no compensation.

    Port and canal disruption

    Red Sea diversions around the Cape of Good Hope added roughly 10 to 14 days to Asia to Europe routes. Panama Canal draft restrictions in drought years affect Asia to US East Coast. Neither is predictable at PO stage, which is the argument for building a buffer rather than a precise plan.

    Customs holds

    Random inspections, classification queries, valuation reviews and missing certificates. Most clear in days. The ones that do not are usually documentation problems that could have been fixed before departure.

    Incoterms decide who eats the delay. Under FOB, risk and cost pass to you once the goods are loaded, so a two week port delay is your problem. Under DDP, the supplier carries it to your door. The term you sign is a delay allocation, not just a price.

    When to pay for air freight

    Air moves the same goods in 5 to 10 days door to door and costs roughly 5 to 15 times more per kilo. The calculation is simple once you frame it as a cost of capital and stockout question rather than a shipping question.

    Air makes sense for high value, low weight, time critical goods, for launch samples, and for the top up shipment that keeps a bestseller in stock while the sea container is in transit. It rarely makes sense for bulky low margin goods, where the freight can exceed the product cost.

    A common structure is to split: 10 to 20 percent by air to open sales, the rest by sea to follow four to six weeks later.

    Planning backwards from the in-stock date

    Pick the date the goods must be sellable, then subtract in this order: 7 days receiving and putaway, 10 days customs and drayage buffer, 35 days ocean transit, 10 days inspection and booking, 45 days production, 14 days sampling and PO. That is 121 days, and it is why the number is what it is.

    For seasonal goods the working back date is usually harder than the lead time, which is why seasonal buyers run on a 150 day planning cycle and place orders the season before the one they are selling into.

    Payment terms across a four month gap

    Standard terms are 30 percent deposit on order and 70 percent against the bill of lading copy. That means most of the money leaves before you have seen the goods, and it is the reason third party inspection before shipment is worth its cost on any order above a few thousand.

    Letters of credit shift risk to the banks but add cost and paperwork, so they mostly appear on large first orders with new suppliers. For repeat suppliers, the deposit structure with a pre shipment inspection is the normal middle ground.

    Cash flow is the hidden cost of a 120 day lead time. Money leaves in month one and returns in month five at the earliest, which is why importers who grow quickly run out of cash while profitable on paper.

    Other 120 day deadlines

    Credit card chargeback rights under Visa and Mastercard rules commonly run 120 days from the transaction or the expected delivery date, which is a useful backstop when a supplier disappears. US Medicare and several insurance programmes use 120 day claim filing windows. Many commercial construction contracts set 120 days for final payment and lien release.

    Shorter shipping promises sit at 60 days for regional freight, and the longest financial and tax windows run to 180 days, which is also the completion deadline for a 1031 property exchange.

    Common questions

    How long does a shipping container take from China to Europe?

    Ocean transit alone is usually 25 to 40 days. Counted from purchase order to warehouse, including production, inspection, customs and inland delivery, 120 days is the common planning figure.

    Why does Chinese New Year affect delivery dates so much?

    Factories close for one to three weeks and output drops in the weeks either side while workers travel. Orders placed shortly before the holiday routinely ship six to eight weeks later than quoted.

    Is air freight worth it to avoid the wait?

    For high value, low weight or time critical goods, often yes. Air is roughly 5 to 15 times the cost per kilo and takes 5 to 10 days door to door, so it rarely suits bulky low margin products.

    Who is responsible for a delay at the port?

    It depends on the Incoterm. Under FOB the buyer carries the risk once goods are loaded. Under DDP the seller carries it until delivery at the buyer's premises.
